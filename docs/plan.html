WorldLibs experience plan

You arrive at a real-looking Earth, built from NASA satellite imagery, with day and night drawn where the sun was in the year on the slider. Give it a nudge and it unrolls into a flat atlas where every library sits as a tiny floor plan of its own room. Pick one and the camera flies there, the plan rises into walls, and your device builds the room while you watch.

Entry screen
A photoreal globe (NASA Blue Marble by day, Black Marble city lights by night) with a time scrubber from 700 BCE to today. It unrolls into an Equal Earth atlas for picking, so the European cluster never hides behind the curve.
First representation
A floor-plan glyph per library, pinned with its lifespan. Outside its dates it fades to a ghost outline. Five shapes cover the whole list, and each shape is one reusable 3D kit, so the legend on the map is the build plan.
The big moment
The fly-in. The camera follows a great circle to the city while the room prefetches, the glyph tilts and extrudes, and a Worker on your device builds the shelves from a seed behind the door.

    Five glyphs, five kits

    The glyph is the library's footprint reduced to its type. The same typology decides how each room gets built: a hall is one bay module repeated down an axis, so the Long Room, Admont and Strahov share most of their geometry and differ in ceilings, wood and paint.

    Worlds built on arrival

    Rooms are not downloaded as finished models. Each one ships as a hall-kit file of a few KB plus a seed, and your device builds the shelves and books in a Worker while the walls rise. Light and fine detail are too slow to make on a phone, so the build server bakes them once and ships them as textures. Roblox splits work the same way: it streams what is near you, shows a baked stand-in (SLIM) for what is far, and can pause a player at the edge of the loaded area until the next part arrives.

    Why each room matters

    A faithful room is only half of it. People remember a place for what it held, who worked there and what happened to it. The Digital Library Cave for Dunhuang put 21 zoomable documents and real historical figures inside a 3D hall and logged 14 million visits in a week. Each WorldLibs room gets the same layers, told from sources.

    Rooms at true scale

    Drawn to one scale. Every room here takes under about a minute to walk at 1.4 m/s, which keeps each one small enough to model and bake by hand, and sets the camera's walking speed. Dashed edges mark a dimension taken from background knowledge.

    Showing how sure we are

    The report grades every reconstruction as known, probable or speculative. The app shows that grade in the rendering itself, so nobody mistakes a guess about Alexandria for a survey of Ephesus. Every lost room gets a toggle between the fact layer and the guess.

    When it stood

    Lost libraries need a sense of time as much as a sense of place. Each one gets four layers: a lifespan with honest, blurry ends, a then-and-now view, the evidence shading above, and shelves that show what survived and what is known to be gone. Tap a row to see its dated events.

    Books: what you can open

    Every book on a shelf gets a record, but only some can be read. The shelf shows which is which before you reach for it: readable spines carry the bright band, catalog books stay closed, and lost works appear as outlines with their title.

    Data budget and caching

    We store pointers, not copies. Our files hold the checked facts and, for every book, its ID at the source. The pages themselves come from the holder's public API. There is no database: everything we host is static files on a CDN, under 1 GB at full size (facts under 2 MB, a globe of 3 to 4 MB, 30 rooms at up to 25 MB each, a few hundred pre-built texts). Copying the scans is what would make it grow, since the First Folio alone is 920 pages, about 440 MB even at screen size, so we never copy them.

    Each step loads only what that step needs, with file names that change when the content changes. The browser keeps files forever and asks again only for the small per-library manifests. A second visit to a room costs one tiny request.

    Texts are built ahead

    Gutenberg is the one source a browser cannot read directly: its files send no CORS header, and it asks people not to fetch its site by script. So a build step pulls each text once from Standard Ebooks or a Gutenberg mirror, cuts it into chapter files of plain paragraphs and puts them on our CDN. Gilgamesh is 250 KB as plain text.

    Pages are laid out on the device

    The text arrives as paragraphs, not pages. The reader paginates for the current screen and font size, so a phone and a desktop share the same files and changing the font never refetches anything.

    Easy to read

    Every book also opens in a plain reader without the 3D room: adjustable type, light, sepia and dark, and page turns that switch to a fade when reduced motion is on. The 3D page turn is the treat, the plain reader is the workhorse.

    Scans stay at the source

    e-codices and the Bodleian both let any site load their IIIF images, so the browser asks them for exactly the size it draws. The only thing we pre-process is the manifest. The First Folio's is 1.2 MB, so a build step cuts it to a page list of a few KB. If a holder ever blocks browsers, we mirror the few pages a room shows, never the whole book.

    Public APIs feed the build, not the facts

    Wikidata, Wikimedia Commons and Open Library all answer browsers directly, and we use them. They fill IDs, cover images and author dates at build time, where the checks compare them with our sources before anything ships. They don't serve facts live: anyone can edit Wikidata, and Gutendex returned 503 while this page was being checked.

    The first second

    Snappy means something is always on screen and every wait is covered by motion. Budgets: 5 MB before the first room, LCP under 3 s, p95 frame time of 20 ms or less. WebGL is the default; WebGPU ships only after an A/B test wins.

    Keeping it accurate

    LibraryFieldWasNow

    What else would make it trustworthy

    1. An ID for everything. A Wikidata ID for each library, book and author, plus the holder's shelfmark and catalogue link, so records can be checked against the source and kept in sync.
    2. Dates as ranges. Store the earliest and latest year with every date and print "c." or a span when they differ. No invented single years for ancient events.
    3. Say how a book relates to the room. Held here, survived from here and now elsewhere, known to be lost, or only associated. The Book of Kells is held in Dublin; a Gilgamesh tablet from Nineveh is held in London.
    4. Licence gate before the reader. The read button appears only when the record has a licence that allows it. Non-commercial scans are flagged so they can be dropped if the project ever earns money.
    5. Name the edition. The title page of every readable book shows the translator, year and source. Partial works say so, for example "volume 1 of 10", and never claim to be complete.
    6. A weekly link check. A scheduled job reruns every check command in the facts file. A dead manifest or text flips its row to failing and hides the read button until it is fixed.
    7. Ask the people who know. Request floor plans from the libraries for the MVP rooms, and have one specialist read each lost-library scene before it ships: an Assyriologist for Nineveh, a papyrologist for Herculaneum.
    8. Report an error. Every card and title page has a link that opens a report with the fact's ID already filled in.
